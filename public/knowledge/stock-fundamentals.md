# Stock Fundamentals

A quick-reference companion for reading earnings reports and deciding what a share is actually worth.

## Core ratios

- **P/E (price-to-earnings)** = share price / EPS. Tells you how many years of current profit you're paying for. High P/E is either a quality/growth premium or a bubble — compare to the company's own 10-year range and to sector peers, not to "the market".
- **Forward P/E** uses analyst-estimated next-year EPS. Useful in fast-growth names but remember analyst estimates are anchors, not truth.
- **PEG** = P/E / EPS growth rate. A rough way to de-bias P/E against growth. PEG < 1 has historically been a Peter Lynch green flag.
- **EPS (earnings per share)** = net income / shares outstanding. Watch the share count trend: buybacks mechanically raise EPS even when net income is flat.
- **Revenue growth** matters more than EPS for early-stage names. At scale, margin expansion matters more than revenue.
- **Profit margins**: gross → operating → net. A rising operating margin is usually a better quality signal than a one-time earnings beat.
- **Market cap** = share price × shares outstanding. It's what you'd pay for the whole business — not assets, not revenue.

## Owner-earnings and DCF basics

Accounting earnings ≠ cash. What Buffett calls **owner earnings** is roughly:

  net income + depreciation − maintenance capex − working-capital needs

That's the cash the business throws off after keeping the lights on. A DCF takes that stream, projects it out 5–10 years, applies a terminal value, and discounts back at a required rate of return (typically 8–12% for equities). The output is a range, not a point estimate — a good DCF tests both the growth rate and the discount rate.

Practical version for a beginner: if a company's **free cash flow yield** (FCF / market cap) beats long-term bonds by 2–3 percentage points **and** the business is durable, that's a reasonable starting point.

## Bull / base / bear framing

Before buying, write three one-paragraph scenarios:

- **Bull**: what has to go right for the stock to double in 3 years?
- **Base**: the "nothing special happens" path.
- **Bear**: what kills this investment? A 40% drawdown scenario.

If you can't articulate the bear case clearly, you don't understand the business yet.

## Buy / hold / sell heuristics

- **Buy**: thesis intact, price meaningfully below your fair-value range, portfolio allocation allows.
- **Hold**: thesis intact, price fair. The expected return is the rate of business compounding; sitting is underrated.
- **Sell**: thesis broken (management turnover, moat eroded, accounting red flags), or price far above even the bull case, or you've concentrated too much (single name > 25% is usually too much for most investors).

## Risk metrics

- **Beta**: how much the stock moves vs the market. Beta > 1 means amplified moves (leveraged to the cycle); < 1 is defensive.
- **Sharpe ratio**: excess return per unit of volatility. Above ~1 is good; above 2 is rare.
- **Max drawdown**: worst peak-to-trough loss over a window. Ask yourself whether you'd hold through a repeat before you buy.

## Red flags to spot in a 10-K

- Revenue growing while receivables grow faster: possible channel stuffing.
- "Non-GAAP" earnings 2× larger than GAAP: question what's being added back.
- Frequent management changes, especially CFO.
- Debt covenants close to being breached.
- Concentrated customer or supplier (one counterparty > 20%).

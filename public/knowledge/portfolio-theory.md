# Portfolio Theory for Individual Investors

Theoretical finance spent decades formalising what practitioners already knew: don't concentrate, and rebalance when the weights drift. The math is Markowitz, the practice is a handful of rules.

## Modern Portfolio Theory, minus the jargon

The one insight worth remembering: **risk is not about individual positions — it's about how they move together**. Two volatile stocks with low correlation can produce a less volatile portfolio than either alone. Formally, portfolio variance depends on position weights, individual variances, and pairwise correlations. Practically, it means a portfolio of 10 uncorrelated bets is safer than 10 bets in the same sector even if each is "diversified by number".

## Diversification, by sector, asset, geography

- **Sector**: US equities cluster hard — tech, consumer discretionary, financials, energy, healthcare each have their own cycles. A portfolio that is 60% one sector is less diversified than the raw number of tickers suggests.
- **Asset class**: stocks, bonds, real estate, cash, and commodities. Historical pairwise correlations are not stable — in 2008 and again in 2022, stocks and bonds fell together. A modest allocation to real assets (REITs, commodities) has historically improved drawdown profiles.
- **Geography**: US equities are ~60% of global market cap. A 100% US portfolio is a concentrated bet on US outperformance. International developed and emerging add decorrelation, at the cost of currency risk.

## Rebalancing strategies

Two approaches, both work:

- **Calendar rebalancing**: once a year or once a quarter, sell winners / buy losers back to your target weights. Simple. Low tax cost if done in tax-advantaged accounts.
- **Threshold rebalancing**: trigger when any weight drifts more than, say, 5 percentage points from target. More responsive, more trades.

The key insight: rebalancing is a **behavioural forcing function**. It makes you sell what's gone up and buy what's down — which is the reverse of what untrained instinct does. Over long periods this contributes modestly to returns and meaningfully to sleep.

## Dollar-cost averaging (DCA)

Investing a fixed dollar amount on a fixed schedule regardless of price. The math on lump-sum vs DCA in rising markets slightly favours lump-sum (markets go up more than down), but DCA wins on **behaviour**: you keep buying in drawdowns instead of freezing. For most people the behavioural edge dominates the mathematical edge.

## Tax-loss harvesting

If a position is down, selling to realise the loss offsets taxable gains elsewhere (up to $3,000 against ordinary income per year in the US, rest carries forward). Two rules:

- **Wash-sale rule**: don't buy back the "substantially identical" security within 30 days before or after the sale. This includes the same ETF in your spouse's IRA.
- Harvest into **similar-but-not-identical** exposure (e.g., sell VTI, buy ITOT) so market exposure stays constant.

Best done at year-end, after you know your total realised gain picture.

## Concentration is the real risk

The fastest way to blow up a portfolio is concentration in one position. If any single holding is over ~20% of net worth, the math of recovery is brutal — a 50% drawdown in a 30% position is a 15% portfolio hit, and it takes roughly a 100% recovery in the name to break even. Trim concentrated winners on the way up, even if the thesis still looks good. The goal is not to maximise upside; it's to survive the downside.

## A default allocation

Not advice — a common starting point for a long horizon: 60–80% global equities, 10–20% bonds, 0–10% real assets, 5–10% cash. Adjust for age, income stability, and your specific liabilities.

# AI in Consulting — What It Replaces and What It Doesn't

Consulting is a knowledge-work value chain: understand a client's problem → design a solution → configure / build → validate → train / handover. AI models help unevenly across that chain. The honest answer is: the middle of the chain is under the most pressure, the edges are safer.

## What LLMs do well (today)

- **Documentation**: test scripts, process documents, runbooks, training decks. 2× to 10× speedup is real.
- **Code and configuration generation**: from ABAP snippets to SAP configuration steps described in natural language to standard integration iflows. Quality is uneven; a senior consultant still reviews everything.
- **First-pass analysis**: requirements summarisation, gap-fit spreadsheets, RAID log drafts, ticket triage.
- **Translation / localisation**: SAP docs in English → multiple languages with reasonable quality.
- **Regression testing scripts**: given a functional spec, LLMs can generate coverage test cases faster than humans can type them.

A consultant using AI tools well is probably 30–50% more productive on these tasks. At team scale that's fewer billable hours per engagement, which compresses revenue for staffing-heavy firms.

## What LLMs don't replace

- **Stakeholder management**: translating a CFO's worry into a prioritised backlog, running workshops, navigating political pushback. The listening half of consulting.
- **Cross-system context**: "this proposed change breaks a batch job that runs on Tuesdays, which affects close week for Finance". That institutional memory is still mostly in humans.
- **Edge-case config decisions**: the difference between a clean go-live and a two-week post-go-live firefight usually comes down to a dozen decisions only an experienced functional lead can call.
- **Accountability**: clients pay consultants not just for work, but for someone to hold responsible. LLMs cannot be held responsible.
- **Regulatory context**: tax codes, audit requirements, industry-specific compliance. LLMs make confident-sounding errors here.

## The shape of consulting that gets replaced first

- **Junior config work** based on copy-paste from existing projects.
- **Repeatable documentation bundles** (BPDs, test scripts, training guides).
- **Report building** and KPI dashboards from known data models.
- **Standard integrations** following well-known patterns.

If your role is "you describe, I configure" with limited decision-making, that's the zone under pressure.

## The shape of consulting that compounds

- **Architects** who hold the big picture across modules and systems.
- **Industry specialists** with deep domain knowledge (utilities, pharma, CPG).
- **Change managers** who can run org transformations, not just system configurations.
- **Integration leads** who own the wire-level contracts between SAP and everything else.
- **Data stewards** who keep master data clean across environments.
- **Solution owners** with client relationships that predate and postdate any single project.

## Practical ways to future-proof

1. **Get closer to the business**, not the system. Learn your client's P&L, their supply chain, their org politics. That knowledge is defensible.
2. **Own outcomes, not activities**. Bid fixed-scope or outcome-based rather than time-and-materials where possible. AI productivity accrues to the seller.
3. **Adopt AI aggressively as a user**, not defensively. The consultants replaced first are the ones still typing when the AI-augmented ones are reviewing.
4. **Build one scarce skill deeply** — e.g., a specific industry template, a rare integration, a regulatory area. Breadth is now cheap; depth still pays.
5. **Write**. Case studies, blog posts, internal playbooks. Credentialing is shifting from years-at-firm to demonstrated artifacts.

## The optimistic take

AI expands the market. Migrations that were uneconomic at old delivery costs become viable, which creates more projects, not fewer — but the share of revenue inside any one project that goes to "bodies typing" shrinks, and the share that goes to architecture and change leadership grows. The trend is not "no more consultants"; it's "fewer, higher-paid, more specialised consultants."

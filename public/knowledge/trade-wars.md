# Trade Wars and Tariffs

A trade war is escalating use of tariffs, quotas, and non-tariff barriers between countries. Since 2018, the dominant axis has been the US and China, with parallel frictions over steel/aluminium, semiconductors, and critical minerals. This note is the framework, not the forecast.

## How a tariff actually works

A tariff is a tax paid by the **importer** at the border. The tax raises the landed cost; the importer, distributor, and retailer decide how much of it to absorb and how much to pass to consumers. Two key facts:

- Empirical studies of the 2018–2020 US-China tariffs found that most of the cost passed through to US consumers, not to Chinese producers.
- Targeted industries (steel, washing machines) saw price rises; domestic producers gained share temporarily; downstream industries (e.g., auto manufacturers using steel) absorbed higher input costs.

## Sector exposures

- **Consumer goods (apparel, footwear, electronics)**: direct tariff hit. Names with heavy China manufacturing — Nike, Apple, Peloton — get dragged on tariff news. Some diversify to Vietnam, India, Mexico; the shift is multi-year.
- **Automakers**: steel/aluminium tariffs raise input costs; retaliatory tariffs on US autos hit exports.
- **Semiconductors**: export controls on advanced chips to China hit the chip makers but also the tools (ASML, Applied Materials, Lam Research). Long lead times on fabs mean effects take years to fully play out.
- **Agriculture**: often the target of retaliation; soy, pork, and dairy saw the biggest hits in 2018–2019.
- **Energy**: commodity-priced, less tariff-sensitive than manufactured goods; geopolitics moves the price more than tariffs do.

## Supply-chain disruption patterns

Three things happen when an industry's primary sourcing country faces tariffs or sanctions:

1. **Re-shoring**: some production returns to the US. Slow and expensive; usually announced in years, delivered in decades.
2. **Friend-shoring**: production shifts to allied countries (Mexico, Vietnam, India). Faster but capacity-constrained.
3. **Inventory buffering**: short term, companies build inventory ahead of tariff deadlines. Q-over-Q inventory swings can mislead margin analysis.

The tell in a company's 10-K: look for discussion of "China +1", "Vietnam capacity", or specific country mix percentages. Companies that disclose concrete diversification plans are usually ahead of peers.

## How sanctions work

Sanctions are different from tariffs: they restrict or forbid specific transactions with specific entities, not tax them. Types:

- **Asset freezes** (individuals, companies)
- **Sectoral sanctions** (finance, energy, defence)
- **Export controls** (specific technologies, like advanced semis)
- **Secondary sanctions** (penalising third parties for dealing with the primary target)

For investors, sanctions have two effects: (1) they remove access to markets (Russia 2022 wiped out foreign investment overnight); (2) they create compliance risk — banks and corporates must screen transactions, which is expensive and error-prone.

## Impact on consumer sectors

- **Discretionary** (apparel, travel, leisure): sensitive to consumer confidence, which tariff headlines can hurt.
- **Staples** (food, household): resilient, but packaging (plastics, metals) can see input-cost pressure.
- **Tech hardware**: most exposed to component cost pass-through; software is insulated.

## Trading the news

Tariffs rarely arrive as a single surprise — they're usually telegraphed by threats, retaliatory announcements, and exemption carve-outs. The largest stock moves cluster on (a) initial announcement, (b) implementation date, (c) deal-or-escalation headlines. Sharp single-day moves on heavy volume often reverse within a week; base-rate awareness helps avoid whipsaws.

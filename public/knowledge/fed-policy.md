# Fed Policy and Markets

The Federal Reserve has a dual mandate: maximum employment and stable prices (2% inflation target). Its main tool is the federal funds rate — the overnight rate at which banks lend reserves to each other. Every other rate in the economy (mortgages, auto loans, corporate bonds) prices off that anchor plus a spread.

## How rate changes flow to asset prices

- **Short-term rates** (T-bills, savings accounts): move almost 1:1 with Fed actions.
- **Long-term rates** (10-year Treasury): driven by expectations of future short rates + term premium. When markets believe the Fed will hike and stay high, long rates rise; when they price in cuts, long rates fall.
- **Equities**: higher rates mean (1) a higher discount rate on future cash flows (hurts long-duration growth names most), (2) more competition from bonds for capital, (3) tighter credit for businesses. The sensitivity is biggest in tech and unprofitable growth; defensives are relatively insulated.
- **Real estate**: mortgage rates move with the 10-year. A 100 bp rise in 30-year mortgage rates reduces what a buyer can afford at a fixed payment by roughly 10%.
- **Dollar**: higher US rates relative to peers → stronger dollar → headwind for multinational earnings translated back to USD.

## Inflation indicators

Three to watch, in order of frequency:

- **CPI** (monthly, BLS): headline and "core" (ex food and energy). Markets react most strongly to core. Imperfect — has lagging shelter component.
- **PCE** (monthly, BEA): the Fed's preferred measure. Core PCE is the one they explicitly target.
- **PPI** (monthly, BLS): producer prices, leads consumer prices by a couple of months.

Labour market: **non-farm payrolls** (first Friday of each month) and **average hourly earnings** are the employment side of the mandate. Wage growth that outpaces productivity sustainably is inflationary.

## Quantitative easing and tightening (QE / QT)

When rates are already low, the Fed can buy assets (Treasuries, agency MBS) to expand its balance sheet. Effect: pushes long-term rates down further and adds liquidity.

**QT** is the reverse — letting the balance sheet shrink by not reinvesting maturing bonds. Effect: withdraws liquidity, tends to raise long rates at the margin.

QE periods historically correlated with strong equity performance; QT periods with choppier markets and compressed multiples. Correlation, not causation, and the sample is small.

## Historical cycles

Pattern-matching by history is tempting and often wrong, but a few regularities:

- **Hiking cycles** last about 12–18 months; the Fed tends to keep rates high for 6–12 months before cutting.
- The biggest equity drawdowns often come **after** the last hike, as the lagged effects hit the economy.
- A recession following a hiking cycle is common but not guaranteed.
- **Soft landings** (disinflation without recession) are rare but not unprecedented (1994–1995).

## Signals that the Fed is pivoting

Watch, roughly in order of lead time:

1. FOMC statement language changes ("further increases may be appropriate" → "proceeding carefully").
2. Dot-plot shifts (quarterly SEP release).
3. Fed Chair press conference tone and Q&A.
4. Testimony on Capitol Hill.

Market pricing (fed funds futures) usually sees it first — the Fed rarely surprises against explicit market pricing by more than 25 bp.

## What to do with this

As an investor, don't try to out-forecast the Fed. Instead:

- Keep enough cash/short bonds to avoid forced selling in drawdowns.
- Recognise that multiples compress in tightening cycles — don't over-pay for growth assuming it'll re-rate.
- Use rate-sensitive drawdowns to buy quality, not to time the bottom exactly.
